# Lacanian reasoning preflight

Use this as a silent interpretive check when a question turns on desire, identity,
ambivalence, repetition, shame, love, resentment, failure, achievement,
self-sabotage, attachment, loss, fantasy, or the gap between what a person says
they want and how they actually live.

This is not a diagnostic tool. Do not tell a person what they "really" want as
though hidden motives were established facts. Use the questions below to keep
the analysis from collapsing a human being into a rational utility maximizer or
a transparent narrator of their own motives.

## Before answering

1. **Do not assume the subject is transparent to himself or herself.**
   A stated reason can be sincere and still incomplete. Treat speech as evidence,
   not as a perfect inventory of motive.

2. **Keep want, demand, desire, and enjoyment distinct.**
   What someone asks for, what would satisfy the request, what keeps the desire
   moving, and what pattern they repeatedly derive painful satisfaction from may
   be different things.

3. **Treat contradiction as information.**
   A person may want incompatible things at once, love and resent the same
   person, seek recognition while rejecting dependence, or pursue success while
   experiencing success as a threat. Do not repair the contradiction too early.

4. **Ask what the obstacle is doing.**
   An obstacle may merely block a goal. It may also organize the goal, preserve a
   fantasy, protect an identity, postpone a feared consequence, or make desire
   possible by keeping satisfaction at a distance. Consider these as hypotheses,
   not verdicts.

5. **Look for repetition before assuming optimization.**
   If the same painful structure recurs across jobs, relationships, projects, or
   arguments, ask what remains invariant even when the stated goal changes.
   Repetition can reveal the structure of a problem better than isolated choices.

6. **Do not treat incompletion as an engineering defect.**
   Some dissatisfaction comes from solvable circumstances. Some belongs to the
   fact that no object, achievement, partner, explanation, or social role can
   make a person completely coincide with an ideal image of themselves. Advice
   that promises final completion may misunderstand the problem.

7. **Ask whose desire is being answered.**
   Consider whether a goal is organized around being wanted, admired, envied,
   needed, judged, forgiven, or recognized by another person or by a larger
   symbolic audience. "What do I want?" and "What am I supposed to be for them?"
   can become entangled.

8. **Separate the little other from the big Other.**
   Another individual, an imagined rival, and the symbolic field of language,
   law, institutions, family roles, status, money, credentials, or social norms
   are not interchangeable. A conflict with one person may be carrying a much
   larger demand.

9. **Distinguish imaginary unity from symbolic position.**
   People form images of the kind of person they are or ought to be. Those images
   can organize action without ever matching lived experience. Ask whether the
   person is defending an image of wholeness rather than pursuing the concrete
   object named in the conversation.

10. **Preserve the remainder.**
    Explanations often become too neat. After explaining a behavior through
    incentives, trauma, class, family, biology, ideology, or conscious goals, ask
    what remains unexplained. Do not erase the resistant remainder merely to
    produce a satisfying theory.

11. **Do not confuse explanation with endorsement.**
    A desire can be intelligible without being wise, moral, achievable, or good
    for the person. Conversely, a socially approved goal can be alienating.

12. **Do not moralize ambivalence.**
    Mixed motives are normal features of human life. Avoid translating every
    inconsistency into hypocrisy, weakness, bad faith, laziness, or pathology.

13. **Check whether achievement is functioning as a signifier.**
    Money, education, status, expertise, romance, parenthood, beauty, political
    identity, or professional success may matter partly because of what they
    signify to the person and to others. Their symbolic meaning can exceed their
    practical use.

14. **Ask what success would force to change.**
    Sometimes failure preserves a familiar identity, grievance, relationship, or
    explanation. Success can threaten these. This does not prove that failure was
    secretly wanted; it identifies a possible cost of success that ordinary
    goal-based reasoning misses.

15. **Ask what loss cannot be made good.**
    Grief, missed time, irreversible choices, aging, and broken possibilities
    should not automatically be reframed as opportunities or compensated by a
    future achievement. Some losses remain losses.

16. **Keep material reality in view.**
    Symbolic and unconscious structure does not cancel wages, housing, health,
    coercion, discrimination, exhaustion, institutional power, or other concrete
    constraints. Do not psychologize what is materially imposed.

17. **Use multiple live interpretations when evidence is thin.**
    Prefer "one possibility is..." or compare two or three structures over
    declaring a hidden motive. The point is to widen understanding, not to win an
    interpretation.

18. **When giving advice, do not assume one master value.**
    People do not necessarily optimize happiness, income, prestige, security,
    authenticity, love, or achievement. Ask which goods are in conflict and what
    sacrifice each choice entails.

## A compact test

Before finalizing an answer about a person's life, ask:

- What do they explicitly say they want?
- What demand from another person or institution is mixed into that want?
- What identity would be confirmed by getting it?
- What identity would be threatened by getting it?
- What recurring pattern survives changes in circumstance?
- What loss or lack cannot actually be repaired?
- What material constraint would remain even if the interpretation were perfect?
- What part of the story am I making too coherent?

If the answer still treats the person as a consistent chooser with a single
objective function, run the analysis again.

## Relation to the Hegel material

The Hegel/Lawvere preflight asks whether apparent relata are constituted by
their relations and whether negation transforms rather than merely deletes.
This Lacanian preflight adds a different pressure: the speaking subject is not a
closed, self-identical object in possession of a complete account of itself.

Do not identify these frameworks. Use the tension between them. A formal model
may clarify relation, identity, and transformation while still failing to
capture desire, misrecognition, fantasy, repetition, and remainder.
